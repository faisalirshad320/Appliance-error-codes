Hisense refrigerator error codes: what the display is telling you

Last reviewed 2026-10-15 · Hisense refrigerator

What it means

Hisense refrigerators show faults as a short code in the temperature display, usually the letter E or F followed by a digit. The great majority of them are sensor faults: a thermistor that has gone open or short circuit, so the control no longer trusts the temperature it is reading and switches to a safe default run pattern. The next most common group is defrost and fan faults, which show up as a fridge that is still cold but slowly losing ground. A dead compressor is rare, and that is the good news: most Hisense codes point at a $20 part rather than a sealed system. The mapping of code to component does vary between model lines, so the list on the rating plate inside your fridge beats any generic chart, including this one.

Safety first

Unplug the refrigerator before removing any interior panel or touching a sensor or wiring connector. Never open, cut or vent the sealed refrigerant circuit, and never attempt a compressor or sealed-system repair yourself; that is licensed work and the line pressures and oil make it dangerous as well as illegal in many places.

Why a Hisense refrigerator shows this code

These are the causes in the order you are most likely to hit them. Work down the list, do not skip to the expensive one.

  1. Temperature sensor (thermistor) faults, typically the E1 and E2 groupmost casesThe fridge is still running, often running constantly, and the compartment temperature is wrong or stuck. A thermistor is a two-wire bead sensor clipped to the liner or the evaporator, and when it goes open or short the control stops believing it and falls back to a timed run pattern. The giveaway is a cabinet that is either far too cold or slowly warming while the compressor sounds perfectly normal.
  2. Defrost system faults, including the defrost sensor and defrost abnormality codescommonThe freezer still freezes but the fresh food compartment creeps warm over a few days, and the air coming out of the vents feels weak. Pull the rear interior panel in the freezer and look at the evaporator coil. A coil packed solid with white frost rather than a light even dusting means defrost is not running, and that blocked coil is what is starving the fridge of cold air.
  3. Fan faults, including the evaporator fan and on convertible models the zone fancommonOpen the freezer door and press the door switch in with a finger: on most models the evaporator fan should spin up within a few seconds and you should hear it clearly. Silence with the compressor running points at the fan. Very often the fan is electrically fine and is frozen into a block of ice from an earlier defrost failure, so look before buying.
  4. Communication faults between the display board and the main boardoccasionalThe code appears with the fridge otherwise behaving normally, or the display acts erratically, blanks or shows segments that make no sense. The usual cause is the ribbon or harness that runs up through the door hinge or the cabinet trim, which flexes every time the door opens and eventually breaks a conductor inside the insulation.
  5. Door and door switch alarms, which are not faults at alloccasionalThe code or alarm appears after the door has been held open, or a door looks shut but the seal is riding over a drawer or a bottle. Press the door switch plunger by hand and watch the interior light go out. A switch that does not act on the light is a switch the control also cannot see, and the fridge will alarm with the door firmly closed.
  6. Compressor, inverter drive or sealed system faultsrareNothing in the cabinet is getting cold at all, the compressor is silent or clicking on and off every few minutes, and the back of the fridge stays at room temperature after an hour. This is the only group on this page that is not a DIY repair, and it is also the least common. Do not reach for it until the sensors, the defrost system and the fans have all been ruled out.

Fix it step by step

  1. Write the code down exactly as it appears

    Photograph the display. On a seven-segment panel an E and an F look different but a zero and a letter O do not, and dF, dO and d0 are three different things across the Hisense range. Note whether the code is in the fridge display or the freezer display too, because on dual-display models that alone tells you which compartment's sensor is complaining.

    3 min

  2. Find the model number and the code list that belongs to it

    The rating plate is usually on the interior side wall of the fresh food compartment, low down, or behind the bottom crisper drawer. Get the full model number from it and look up the manual for that exact model. Hisense assigns codes differently across model lines, and generic charts found online contradict each other, so five minutes finding the right list saves you from replacing the wrong part.

    10 minTools: Phone camera

  3. Rule out the things that are not faults

    Check the door alarm first: look for a shelf, a drawer or a tall bottle holding a door fractionally open, and press each door switch plunger by hand to confirm the interior light responds. Then make sure the fridge is not in a showroom or demo mode, which units bought from a display floor sometimes arrive in and which stops the compressor running while the display looks perfectly normal. Your manual gives the exit sequence for your model.

    10 min

  4. Do a full five minute power reset

    Unplug the refrigerator at the wall, not at a switch, and leave it for five full minutes before plugging it back in. The control holds the stored fault and a quick off-and-on frequently does not clear it. This also resets the compressor's internal protection timer. If the code comes straight back, it is live rather than a one-off glitch, and that is useful information in itself.

    10 min

  5. Pull the rear freezer panel and look at the evaporator

    Unplug the fridge, empty the freezer, and take out the shelves and the rear interior panel, which is usually a few screws behind plastic caps plus some clips. Behind it is the evaporator coil, the fan and the defrost heater. A light even frost is normal. A solid white block filling the fin pack is a failed defrost, and it also explains any fan noise, weak airflow and warm fresh food compartment at the same time.

    40 minTools: Phillips screwdriver, nut driver, towels

    Unplug before removing the panel. The defrost heater element sits in the coil and is both hot in use and electrically live.

  6. Defrost the cabinet manually before condemning anything

    If the coil is iced, unplug the fridge, move the food to a cooler and leave both doors open with towels down for eight to twenty-four hours, or run a fan into the compartment to speed it up. Never chip ice off with a screwdriver or a knife; the aluminium evaporator tubing punctures easily and that turns a $30 repair into a scrapped refrigerator. Once clear, run it and see how long before the frost returns.

    60 minTools: Towels, cooler, fan

    Never use a sharp tool or a heat gun on an evaporator coil. Puncturing the tubing releases the refrigerant charge and ends the appliance.

  7. Measure the suspect sensor

    With the fridge unplugged, unclip the thermistor the code points at, separate its connector and measure across the two terminals with a multimeter on ohms. You are looking for a sensible, stable reading that changes as the sensor warms in your hand, rather than an open circuit or a dead short, which are the two ways these fail. For the exact expected figure at a given temperature, use the service literature for your model rather than a generic table.

    30 minTools: Multimeter, Phillips screwdriver

    Refrigerator unplugged before touching any sensor or connector.

How to reset it

Unplug the refrigerator at the wall socket, wait a full five minutes, then plug it back in. Five minutes matters: the control board holds residual charge and the compressor has its own protection delay, so a quick off and on usually leaves the stored code in place. After restoring power, give the fridge several hours before judging whether the fault is gone, because a cabinet that has warmed up takes time to recover and will look wrong long after the actual fault is fixed.

It still shows the code

If the reset did not clear it and you have confirmed the code against the list for your actual model number, work in this order, because it runs from free to expensive. First, rule out airflow. A fridge stuffed to the back wall, or with the rear vents in the fresh food compartment blocked by a tall container, produces warm-compartment symptoms and sensor codes that are entirely self-inflicted. Pull everything away from the vents and give it twelve hours. Second, check the condenser coils and the condenser fan at the back or underneath. A coil furred with dust and pet hair makes the compressor run constantly and the cabinet drift warm, and vacuuming it costs nothing. Third, look at the evaporator behind the rear freezer panel. This single check distinguishes the three most common Hisense fault families at once: a solid ice block means the defrost system, a fan frozen into that ice means the fan code is secondary rather than primary, and a clean coil with a warm cabinet sends you back to the sensors. Fourth, measure the thermistor the code names. These are cheap parts, usually $15 to $40, and on most models they clip into the liner with a connector a few inches away, so the swap is a realistic home job once you have identified the right one. Fifth, if the code is a communication fault, inspect the harness where it flexes through the door hinge or the cabinet trim, because a broken conductor inside intact-looking insulation is the common cause and is a repair rather than a board. Only then consider the main control board. And finally, if nothing in the cabinet is getting cold at all, the compressor is silent or short-cycling and the back of the fridge is at room temperature after an hour of running, stop. That is a sealed-system or inverter fault, it is the one group on this list that is not a DIY repair, and on a fridge out of warranty the quote will often be close enough to replacement cost that it is worth asking for both numbers before you commit.

Differences between models

Hisense code assignments are not consistent across the range. The broad pattern holds everywhere, with E codes mostly sensor and system faults and d codes mostly door and defrost states, but the specific digit attached to a specific component moves between the French door, quad door, side by side and single door lines, and between model years. Published charts on third-party repair sites openly contradict each other on several codes. Convertible-zone models add codes of their own for the zone fan and damper that do not exist on a plain two-door fridge. Hisense also manufactures refrigerators sold under other brand names, so a list that looks right for a similar cabinet may belong to a different control board entirely. Use the manual for your own model number, and where the manual and a web chart disagree, believe the manual.

When to stop and call someone

Call a technician if nothing in the cabinet is cooling, the compressor is silent or clicking on and off in short bursts, and the condenser at the back stays at room temperature, because that is the sealed system or the inverter drive and it is not a home repair at any skill level. Call as well if you find oil residue or a sweet smell near the pipework, which indicates a refrigerant leak. Short of that, most Hisense codes are worth a look yourself: sensors, fans and defrost parts are inexpensive and reachable. Do call if the fridge is still under warranty, since opening the cabinet yourself can void it, and Hisense warranty service is free at that point. And if you are weighing a sealed-system quote on an out-of-warranty fridge, ask for the repair price and the replacement price side by side before deciding, because they are frequently closer than people expect.

Questions people ask

What do E1 and E2 mean on a Hisense refrigerator?

On most Hisense models they are sensor faults, with E1 pointing at the fresh food compartment thermistor and E2 at the freezer thermistor, in each case open or short circuit. The fridge usually keeps running on a timed fallback pattern, so it stays cold but the temperature control goes vague. The exact assignment varies by model line, so confirm against your own manual.

Can I still use my Hisense refrigerator with an error code showing?

Usually yes, for a while. Most Hisense codes are sensor or fan faults, and the control keeps the compressor running on a default pattern rather than shutting down. Monitor the actual temperature with a separate thermometer rather than trusting the display, and move anything genuinely perishable if the fresh food compartment is reading above about 40 degrees Fahrenheit.

Will unplugging the fridge clear the code?

Sometimes, and it is always worth trying. Unplug at the wall for a full five minutes, then restore power and wait several hours before judging the result. A code that clears and never returns was a glitch. A code that comes straight back is a live fault and the reset has told you something useful, which is that the problem is still physically present.

Why is my Hisense freezer fine but the fridge section warm?

That pattern almost always means air is not getting from the freezer evaporator into the fresh food compartment. The two usual causes are an evaporator coil iced solid because defrost has failed, and an evaporator fan that has stopped or frozen. Pull the rear freezer panel and look: what you see there answers the question in about thirty seconds.

Why do online Hisense code lists disagree with each other?

Because Hisense genuinely assigns codes differently across its model lines and years, and most published lists are compiled from one or two models and then presented as universal. There is no single master table that is right for every Hisense fridge. The manual or service sheet for your exact model number is the only list that is reliably correct for your machine.

Is a Hisense refrigerator with an error code worth repairing?

Usually, because the common faults are cheap. A thermistor is $15 to $40 and a fan motor $25 to $70, and even with a technician the bill is a fraction of a replacement. The exception is a sealed-system or compressor fault, where the quote on an out-of-warranty fridge often lands close to the price of a new one. Ask for both numbers.

My Hisense fridge shows a code but feels perfectly cold. Should I worry?

Not immediately, but do not ignore it. A sensor fault frequently leaves the fridge running on a timed default that happens to keep it cold today and drifts tomorrow, and a defrost fault takes days to show as warming while ice quietly builds behind the freezer panel. Put a thermometer in each compartment and check it over a week while you arrange the repair.

Is my fridge just in demo mode?

It is worth ruling out, especially on a unit bought from a shop floor or recently power-cycled during a move. Showroom or demo mode lights the display and runs the fans while leaving the compressor off, so the fridge looks alive and never gets cold. The exit sequence differs by model and is printed in the manual, usually as a button combination held for several seconds.
