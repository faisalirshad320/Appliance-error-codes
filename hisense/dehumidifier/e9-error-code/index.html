Hisense dehumidifier E9 error code: what it means and what to do

Last reviewed 2026-10-15 · Hisense dehumidifier

What it means

E9 on a Hisense dehumidifier means the control board is not getting a believable reading from one of its sensors, so it shuts the compressor down rather than run blind. It is a sensing fault, not a full bucket and not a blocked filter. Hisense does not publish a clear definition of this code, and owners who have chased it report the temperature or humidity sensor and its plug far more often than anything else. Start with a five-minute power cycle, because a genuine one-off glitch will clear there and cost you nothing.

Safety first

Unplug the unit at the wall before any screw comes out of the cabinet. A dehumidifier is a sealed refrigeration appliance: the compressor, the coils and the pipework between them are under pressure and are not user-serviceable. Clean the coils and reseat plugs if you like, but do not cut, bend or disconnect any refrigerant line.

Why a Hisense dehumidifier shows E9

These are the causes in the order you are most likely to hit them. Work down the list, do not skip to the expensive one.

  1. A loose or corroded plug on the temperature or humidity sensormost casesWith the unit unplugged and the cabinet open, find the thin two-wire sensor lead clipped to the coil or sitting in the air stream and follow it back to the board. A plug that lifts off with almost no resistance, or shows green or white crust on the pins, is your fault. Damp basements do this constantly.
  2. The sensor itself has drifted or failed opencommonThe plug is clean and tight, the unit still throws E9, and it does so within minutes of starting every single time rather than randomly. A sensor that has failed open reads as an impossible value the instant the board polls it, so the fault is repeatable to the minute.
  3. A one-off control glitch after a power eventcommonThe code appeared after a storm, a brownout, or someone switching the breaker. It clears on a proper five-minute power cycle and does not come back over the following week. If it returns within an hour, this is not what you have.
  4. The coil is iced or the filter is choked, so the sensor reads nonsenseoccasionalPull the filter and look at the coil behind it. A gray felt mat on the filter, or frost and ice on the fins, means airflow has collapsed and the sensor is sitting in air that is nothing like room air. Running a dehumidifier in a cold room is the usual way this happens.
  5. The fan is not turning, or the board is not seeing it turnoccasionalListen at startup. If the fan does not spin up at all, or spins and stops, the board loses its reference and some code tables list E9 as a fan feedback fault on these units. Check nothing is jammed in the blade before you blame the motor.
  6. The control board itself has failedrareA new sensor was fitted and E9 came straight back, which is a pattern several owners report. At that point the board is not reading the sensor correctly rather than the sensor being wrong, and on a consumer dehumidifier this is usually where the repair stops making financial sense.

Fix it step by step

  1. Do a proper five-minute power cycle

    Press the power button off, then pull the plug out of the wall socket and leave it out for five full minutes. Do not use a switched extension lead for this, and do not just press the button. Plug it straight back into a wall socket and start it on continuous. This clears a genuine one-off glitch and costs nothing, so it goes first every time.

    7 min

  2. Check the room temperature before you blame the unit

    Put a thermometer in the room. A dehumidifier working in a cold space ices its coil, airflow collapses, and the sensors end up reading conditions the board does not expect. If the room is cold enough to see your breath, move the unit somewhere warmer for a few hours and try again. This is worth ruling out, but be clear that a genuinely faulty sensor will throw E9 in a warm room too.

    10 minTools: Room thermometer

  3. Pull and wash the air filter

    Slide the filter out of the back or side grille. Rinse it under a warm tap until water runs clear through the mesh, shake it off and let it dry fully before it goes back. A filter that looks lightly dusty can still be half blocked with the fine gray felt that builds in a basement. Never run the unit with the filter out; the coil loads up with dust instead.

    10 min

  4. Let the coil thaw if there is any frost on it

    Look through the grille at the fins behind the filter. If you can see white frost or a sheet of ice, unplug the unit and leave it standing in a warm room for two to three hours with the bucket out, and put a towel under it. Never pick, scrape or chip ice off the fins; they are thin aluminum wrapped around refrigerant tubing and they puncture easily.

    150 minTools: Towel

    Do not use a hairdryer, heat gun or any sharp tool on the coil.

  5. Seat the bucket properly and check the float

    Pull the bucket right out and push it back until it stops square in the opening. Look for the float, the small plastic paddle or foam block that the bucket pushes up, and move it by hand to see that it swings freely rather than sticking on scale or algae. A half-seated bucket does not usually cause E9 specifically, but it causes codes that get mistaken for it.

    5 min

  6. Open the cabinet and reseat the sensor connector

    Unplug the unit. Take out the screws around the back panel, usually six to eight Phillips screws around the edge and sometimes two under the bucket lip, and lift the panel away. Find the thin two-wire sensor lead clipped into the air stream or taped to the coil, follow it to its plug on the control board, pull it off, look at the pins for corrosion, and push it firmly back on. Check the wire has not been chafed through where it passes the chassis.

    35 minTools: Phillips #2 screwdriver

    Unplug at the wall first. Do not touch, bend or lean on the copper refrigerant pipework or the compressor while you are in there.

  7. Decide whether to spend anything on it

    Price a replacement sensor for your model number before you order one. Owners report mixed results: some clear E9 with a new sensor, others fit one and get the same code back within the week, which points at the board instead. Weigh a $20 to $35 part and an hour of your time against the price of a new unit, especially if yours is past three or four years old.

    15 min

How to reset it

Press the power button to switch the unit off, then unplug it from the wall socket, not from a switched strip, and leave it unplugged for five full minutes. Plug it back into a wall socket directly and run it on continuous mode rather than a humidity setpoint, so it has to start the compressor and you find out quickly. Some owners report holding the power button for twenty seconds while unplugged; it does no harm, but there is no evidence it does more than the wait itself. If E9 returns within the hour, the reset has done its job and told you the fault is real.

It still shows E9

If a five-minute power cycle, a clean filter, a thawed coil and a reseated sensor plug have not shifted it, be realistic about where this goes next. Work out first which pattern you have, because it decides everything. If E9 appears within a few minutes of every start, in a warm room, with clean airflow, the sensor circuit is reading an impossible value and the fault is real and repeatable. That is the case where a replacement sensor is worth the $20 to $35 gamble, and you order it against your exact model number rather than a generic part. If instead the unit runs for an hour or more and then drops to E9, and does it again after every reset, that is the pattern owners most often describe and it is the least encouraging one, because it usually means a sensor that drifts as it warms or a board that loses the reading under load. A new sensor fixes some of those and not others. If you have already fitted a new sensor and E9 came straight back, stop: the board is not reading correctly, a replacement board for a consumer dehumidifier typically costs more than the machine did, and no amount of further resetting will help. The honest position on this code is that Hisense publishes no clear definition of E9 for its dehumidifiers, the manual supplied with the unit does not explain it, and the third-party code tables disagree with each other, some calling it a temperature sensor fault and others a fan feedback fault. Do not let anyone sell you a repair based on certainty that does not exist in the documentation. One thing worth knowing before you spend: these units often carry a longer sealed-system warranty than the general one, so find your purchase date and check the terms before you buy a part or throw it out.

Differences between models

Hisense uses E9 across several dehumidifier sizes, and the documentation does not improve with the bigger units. Be careful with code lists written for Hisense air conditioners, which show up first in most searches: E9 on those relates to indoor and outdoor unit communication, which does not exist on a dehumidifier and does not transfer. Note also that the low-temperature defrost condition on these units is generally shown as P2 rather than E9, so if a guide tells you E9 just means the unit is defrosting and will clear itself once warm, treat that with suspicion. Hisense also builds dehumidifiers sold under other brand names, so a code table for a visually identical unit from another label is often more accurate than a generic Hisense one. The screw layout and which panel comes off first varies between the bucket-front and top-grille cabinet styles.

When to stop and call someone

For most people the honest answer is that you do not call anyone for this. A consumer dehumidifier carries a service call of $90 to $180 before parts, which on a unit that cost two or three hundred dollars new rarely makes sense. Before you give up, check the warranty: these machines frequently come with a longer sealed-system warranty than the general parts warranty, so find the purchase date and model number and call Hisense support directly, because a warranty claim is free where a repair is not. Call a technician rather than opening it yourself if the unit is still under cover and you would void it, or if there is any sign of oily residue on the pipework or the floor beneath, which means refrigerant has escaped and the machine is finished as a DIY job.

Questions people ask

Does E9 mean my dehumidifier bucket is full?

No. A full bucket has its own indicator and usually its own code, and the unit stops politely rather than throwing a fault. E9 is a sensing fault: the board is not getting a reading it believes from the temperature or humidity sensor. Emptying and reseating the bucket is still worth thirty seconds, but it is not the cause here.

Will E9 clear by itself if I leave the unit alone?

Sometimes, if the cause was a one-off power event, and a five-minute unplug is the fastest way to find out. Do not count on it. Owners most commonly report the unit running for about an hour after a reset and then showing E9 again, which means the underlying fault is still there and resetting is only clearing the memory.

Can running the dehumidifier in a cold room cause E9?

Indirectly, yes. In a cold room the coil ices up, airflow collapses and the sensors sit in air nothing like the room's real condition, which can produce readings the board rejects. Moving the unit somewhere warmer is worth trying because it is free. But a genuinely failed sensor throws E9 in a warm room too, so this is a test, not a cure.

What does Hisense officially say E9 means?

Very little, and that is the frustrating part. There is no clear published definition for the dehumidifier range, owners report the supplied manual does not explain it, and the third-party code tables contradict each other between a temperature sensor fault and a fan feedback fault. Treat any source that sounds completely certain about this code with caution.

Is it worth replacing the humidity sensor?

It is a reasonable gamble at $15 to $35 if the unit is fairly new and the code appears within minutes of every start. Be aware that several owners have fitted a new sensor and had E9 return immediately, which points at the control board instead. Price the part against your exact model number before ordering, and check your warranty first.

Can I keep using the dehumidifier with E9 showing?

Not usefully. The board shuts the compressor down when it throws this code, so the fan may run but no water will come out of the air. There is no safety hazard in leaving it plugged in, but it is doing nothing for your damp problem. Unplug it and deal with the fault or replace the unit.

Why does E9 come back an hour after every reset?

Because the reset clears the stored fault but not its cause, and something in the sensing circuit only misbehaves once the machine has warmed up and run for a while. That pattern usually points at a sensor drifting with temperature or a marginal connection expanding as it heats. Reseating the sensor plug is the one free thing left to try.
