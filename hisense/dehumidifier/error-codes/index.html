Hisense dehumidifier error codes: what they mean and what to do

Last reviewed 2026-10-15 · Hisense dehumidifier

What it means

Hisense dehumidifiers use two families of code. E codes with a number, such as E1, E2 or E9, are sensor and component faults, and the unit shuts the compressor down rather than run on a reading it does not trust. P codes, such as P1 and P2, are generally protection and bucket conditions rather than failures, and those often clear on their own. The honest position on this brand is that the documentation is poor: Hisense does not publish a clear code list for its dehumidifiers, owners report the supplied manual does not explain the codes, and the third-party tables contradict each other. Start with a five-minute unplug and a washed filter, because those cost nothing and fix a real share of them.

Safety first

Unplug the unit at the wall before any screw comes out of the cabinet. A dehumidifier is a sealed refrigeration appliance: the compressor, the coils and all the pipework between them run under pressure and are not user-serviceable at any skill level. Clean the coils and reseat plugs if you want to, but never cut, bend, lean on or disconnect a refrigerant line. If you find oily residue on the pipework or the floor beneath, the unit has lost refrigerant and is finished as a repair project.

Why a Hisense dehumidifier shows this code

These are the causes in the order you are most likely to hit them. Work down the list, do not skip to the expensive one.

  1. Bucket and float conditions, usually the P code familymost casesThe unit stops and the bucket indicator is lit, or a P code shows. Pull the bucket right out and push it back until it seats square, then find the float, the plastic paddle or foam block the bucket lifts, and move it by hand. Scale or algae holding it in position reads as a full bucket forever.
  2. Choked filter and collapsed airflowmost casesPull the filter from the back or side grille and hold it to the light. A gray felt mat across the mesh, or no light through it, means airflow has collapsed. This causes sensor codes indirectly, because the sensors end up sitting in air nothing like the room's real condition, and it ices the coil in the process.
  3. Sensor faults, the E code family including E9, E1 and E2commonThe board is not getting a believable reading from a temperature or humidity sensor, so it stops the compressor. Published tables variously describe these as temperature sensor, humidity sensor or fan feedback faults and do not agree. Behaviourally they look the same: the code appears shortly after start, repeatedly, and the fan may still run while nothing is dehumidified.
  4. A loose or corroded sensor connectorcommonWith the unit unplugged and the back panel off, find the thin two-wire sensor lead clipped into the air stream or taped to the coil and follow it to the board. A plug that lifts off with almost no resistance, or green or white crust on the pins, is the cause. Basements and damp garages, which is where these units live, do this constantly.
  5. Low room temperature icing the coilcommonLook through the grille at the fins behind the filter. White frost or a sheet of ice means the unit has been working in a room too cold for it. On these units the low-temperature defrost condition is generally reported as P2 rather than as an E code, so treat a guide that tells you E9 just means defrosting with suspicion.
  6. A one-off control glitch after a power eventoccasionalThe code appeared after a storm, a brownout or a breaker reset, clears on a proper five-minute unplug, and does not return over the following week. If it comes back within the hour, which is what owners most commonly report, this is not what you have.
  7. Control board failurerareA replacement sensor was fitted and the same code came straight back, a pattern several owners describe. At that point the board is not reading correctly rather than the sensor being wrong, and on a consumer dehumidifier a replacement board usually costs more than the machine did.

Fix it step by step

  1. Do a proper five-minute power cycle

    Press the power button off, pull the plug out of the wall socket rather than switching off a power strip, and leave it out for five full minutes. Plug it back directly into a wall socket and run it on continuous rather than a humidity setpoint, so the compressor has to start and you learn the answer quickly. This clears a genuine one-off and costs nothing, so it goes first every time.

    7 min

  2. Pull and wash the air filter

    Slide the filter out of the grille on the back or side. Rinse it under a warm tap until water runs clear through the mesh, shake it off and leave it to dry fully before it goes back in. A filter that looks lightly dusty can still be half blocked by the fine gray felt that builds up in a basement. Never run the unit with the filter removed, because the coil loads up with dust instead and that is much harder to clean.

    10 min

  3. Seat the bucket and free the float

    Pull the bucket all the way out and push it back until it stops square in the opening. Look for the float, the small plastic paddle or foam block the bucket pushes up, and move it by hand to check it swings freely rather than sticking on scale or algae. Wash the bucket out with warm soapy water while it is out; the slime that grows in a damp bucket is what jams floats in the first place.

    10 min

  4. Check the room temperature and the coil for ice

    Put a thermometer in the room. These units ice up in cold spaces, airflow collapses and odd codes follow. Look through the grille at the fins: if you see frost or a sheet of ice, unplug the unit and leave it standing in a warm room for two to three hours with the bucket out and a towel underneath. Never pick, scrape or chip at the fins; they are thin aluminum wrapped around refrigerant tubing.

    150 minTools: Room thermometer, towel

    No hairdryer, heat gun or sharp tool anywhere near the coil.

  5. Check the drain hose if you run it continuously

    If you have fitted the continuous drain hose instead of using the bucket, pull it off and look into the outlet spigot, then run water through the hose in a sink. Lint and biofilm narrow these hoses over a season. Check the hose runs steadily downhill the whole way with no sag holding standing water, and that the far end is not submerged in whatever it drains into.

    15 min

  6. Open the cabinet and reseat the sensor connector

    Unplug the unit first. Take out the screws around the back panel, usually six to eight Phillips screws around the edge and occasionally two hidden under the bucket lip, and lift the panel away. Find the thin two-wire sensor lead clipped into the air stream or taped to the coil, follow it to its plug on the board, pull it off, inspect the pins for corrosion and push it firmly back on. Check the wire is not chafed where it passes the chassis.

    35 minTools: Phillips #2 screwdriver, flashlight

    Unplug at the wall first, and do not touch, bend or lean on the copper pipework or the compressor while you are inside the cabinet.

  7. Check the warranty before you spend anything

    Find the model number and serial number on the label at the back or under the bucket, and dig out your purchase date. These units frequently carry a longer sealed-system warranty than the general parts warranty, so a claim may cost you nothing where a repair will not. Call Hisense support with those numbers before you buy a part or write the machine off.

    20 min

How to reset it

Switch the unit off at its own button, then unplug it from a wall socket rather than a switched extension lead, and leave it unplugged for five full minutes. Plug it back directly into the wall and run it on continuous mode rather than a humidity setpoint, so the compressor is forced to start and you find out quickly whether the fault is still there. Some owners hold the power button down for twenty seconds while the unit is unplugged; it does no harm but there is no evidence it achieves more than the wait itself. If a code returns within the hour, the reset has done its job by proving the fault is real rather than a one-off glitch.

It still shows the code

Be straight with yourself about the economics here before going further, because this is a category where the repair often costs more than the machine. If the code survives a five-minute unplug, a washed filter, a freed float and a thawed coil, work out which pattern you have. A code that appears within minutes of every start, in a warm room with clean airflow, means the fault is real and repeatable, and a sensor circuit is reading something impossible. That is the case where a replacement sensor at $15 to $35 is a reasonable gamble, ordered against your exact model number rather than a generic part. A code that lets the unit run for an hour or more and then appears, every time, after every reset, is the pattern owners describe most often and it is the least encouraging one: it suggests a sensor that drifts as it warms or a connection that opens up under heat, and a new sensor fixes some of those and not others. A code that returns immediately after a new sensor has been fitted means the board is not reading correctly, and that is the end of the sensible repair road, because a board for a consumer dehumidifier typically costs more than the unit did new. The one free thing left at any stage is the connector: unplug the machine, get the back panel off and reseat every plug on the control board, not just the sensor one. Damp basements corrode pins and these units live in damp basements by definition. Two honest warnings about researching this yourself. First, most of the Hisense code tables you will find online are written for Hisense air conditioners, where the same code numbers mean completely different things involving indoor and outdoor unit communication that does not exist on a dehumidifier. Check what appliance a page is actually about. Second, the published dehumidifier tables openly contradict each other, so treat any source that sounds completely certain about a specific code with suspicion, and do not let a repair be sold to you on a confidence the documentation does not support.

Differences between models

The single most useful thing to know about Hisense dehumidifier codes is that the documentation is genuinely poor, and that is not a failure of your searching. Hisense does not publish a clear dehumidifier code list, owners report the manual supplied in the box does not explain what the codes mean, and the third-party tables that fill the gap disagree with one another, variously describing the same code as a temperature sensor fault, a humidity sensor fault or a fan feedback fault. Be especially careful with Hisense air conditioner code tables, which dominate search results: the same code numbers on an air conditioner refer to communication between indoor and outdoor units, which a dehumidifier does not have. Hisense also manufactures dehumidifiers sold under other brand names, so a code table for a visually identical unit under a different label is often more accurate than a generic Hisense one. Cabinet styles differ too, with bucket-front and top-grille designs taking apart in different orders, so find the model number on the label at the back or under the bucket before following any disassembly guide.

When to stop and call someone

For most people the honest answer is that you do not call anyone about a dehumidifier. A service call runs $90 to $180 before parts, which on a unit that cost two or three hundred dollars new rarely makes sense, and a technician will reach the same dead end you do if the board is at fault. The one call genuinely worth making is to Hisense support with your model number, serial number and purchase date, because these units frequently carry a longer sealed-system warranty than the general parts warranty and a covered claim is free. Call a technician instead of opening the cabinet yourself if the unit is still in warranty and opening it would void the cover. Stop entirely and replace the unit if you find oily residue on the pipework or the floor beneath it, which means refrigerant has escaped, or if you can smell burning or see scorching inside the cabinet.

Questions people ask

What do E codes and P codes mean on a Hisense dehumidifier?

E codes with a number are component and sensor faults, and the unit shuts the compressor down rather than work from a reading it does not trust. P codes are generally protection and bucket conditions rather than failures, and they often clear on their own once the condition passes. As a rule, a P code is something to wait out and an E code is something to investigate.

Why is there no clear Hisense dehumidifier code list?

Because Hisense does not publish one for this product line. Owners report the manual in the box does not define the codes, and the third-party tables that fill the gap disagree with each other on what the same code means. This is a genuine documentation gap, not a failure of your searching, so be wary of any source that sounds completely certain.

Does a Hisense dehumidifier code mean the bucket is full?

Only if it is in the P family or the bucket indicator is lit alongside it. A full bucket normally stops the unit politely with its own light rather than throwing a fault code. That said, pulling the bucket out, washing it and checking the float moves freely takes two minutes and rules out a sticking float, which can mimic other problems.

Will a Hisense dehumidifier code clear itself?

A P code often will, once the condition that caused it passes. An E code usually will not. A five-minute unplug is the fastest way to find out which you have: if the unit then runs for a week without complaint, it was a one-off. If the code returns within the hour, which is what owners most often report, the fault is real.

Can running the unit in a cold room cause error codes?

Yes, indirectly. In a cold room the coil ices over, airflow collapses and the sensors sit in air nothing like the room's real condition, which produces readings the board rejects. Moving the unit somewhere warmer is worth trying because it is free. But a genuinely failed sensor throws its code in a warm room too, so this is a test rather than a cure.

Is a Hisense dehumidifier worth repairing?

Rarely, once you are past the free checks. A sensor at $15 to $35 is a reasonable gamble on a fairly new unit. A control board at $70 to $160 usually costs more than the machine is worth, and a service call at $90 to $180 almost always does. Check the warranty first, because a covered claim changes the arithmetic entirely.

Are Hisense air conditioner codes the same as dehumidifier codes?

No, and this trips up a lot of people because air conditioner tables dominate the search results. On an air conditioner the same code numbers often refer to communication between the indoor and outdoor units, which a dehumidifier does not have. Always check which appliance a code page is actually written for before acting on it.
