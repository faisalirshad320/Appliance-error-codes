Panasonic microwave H97 error code: what it means and what to do

Last reviewed 2026-10-15 · Panasonic microwave

What it means

H97 on a Panasonic microwave is a self-diagnostic code from the side of the oven that produces the microwaves, which on an inverter model means the inverter board and the magnetron. The documentation is split and you should know that up front: third-party repair references call it an inverter or magnetron circuit failure, while Panasonic's own troubleshooting material lists H97 alongside H98 under a condition where the cavity is too hot from previous use to accept an automatic program. One test separates them, which is whether the oven still cooks when you set a time and power level by hand. Either way, the cabinet stays shut.

Safety first

Do not take the case off a microwave. The high-voltage capacitor inside holds a charge that can kill, and it stays charged after the oven is unplugged. Nothing on this page needs the cabinet opened, and that is deliberate: discharging that capacitor safely takes the right tool and the knowledge of where to apply it. This is one of the few appliance repairs where having a go is genuinely the wrong call. Keep the oven unplugged when you are not actively testing it, and stop using it altogether if the door, its hinges or its seal are damaged.

Why a Panasonic microwave shows H97

These are the causes in the order you are most likely to hit them. Work down the list, do not skip to the expensive one.

  1. A fault in the inverter or magnetron circuitmost casesThe oven runs through a cycle but the food comes out cold, or it starts and stops within seconds and redisplays the code. Put a mug of cold tap water in and run one minute at full power. Water absorbs microwaves efficiently, so it should be clearly warm. Still cold means nothing is being generated and this is the reading that fits.
  2. The cavity is too hot to accept an automatic programcommonPanasonic's own troubleshooting material groups this code with that condition. The check is whether manual cooking still works: set a time and a power level by hand rather than using a sensor, reheat or popcorn program. If manual runs fine and only the automatic programs are refused, nothing has failed and half an hour with the door open resolves it.
  3. Blocked vents causing the oven to overheat itselfcommonThe oven cooks normally from cold and throws the code after ten minutes of use. Look at the grilles on the back, sides and top for dust or obstruction, and check nothing is stored on top and that there is a clear gap behind. An oven recycling its own waste heat will keep doing this no matter how many times you reset it.
  4. A failed cooling fan leaving the magnetron uncooledoccasionalYou hear no fan note at all during a cycle, only the turntable. Or the oven runs for shorter and shorter periods each time before quitting, which is what accumulating heat looks like as the magnetron protection trips sooner on each attempt.
  5. A control fault raising the code with no real hardware failurerareThe water test shows the oven heating properly, the cavity is cold, the vents are clear, and H97 appears regardless. That combination eliminates the readings above and leaves the control board, which is uncommon but not unheard of.

Fix it step by step

  1. Unplug it and let the cavity cool

    Pull the plug at the socket rather than pressing stop, and leave the door open for at least thirty minutes. If the overheat reading is what you have, this is the whole fix, and it also clears a stored transient fault. It costs nothing but time and resolves a genuine share of these, so do it before anything else even though it feels too simple.

    35 min

    Unplug at the socket, and do not remove the outer case at any stage. The high-voltage capacitor holds a lethal charge even with the oven unplugged.

  2. Think about what the oven had just been doing

    A run of back-to-back meals, a long defrost straight into a cook, or the grill or browning function on a combination model all leave the cavity hot. If the code turned up after heavy use rather than out of nowhere on a cold oven, that points toward the overheat reading. If it appeared on the first use of the day, it does not, and you can expect the water test to fail.

    5 min

  3. Set a manual cook instead of an automatic program

    This is the test that separates the two published meanings, and it takes a minute. Plug the oven in and set a short cook by choosing a time and a power level directly, avoiding any sensor, reheat, popcorn or other preset. If manual cooking runs and only the automatic programs refuse, the oven works and the control is declining a program it cannot judge on a hot cavity. If manual fails too, you are on the hardware side.

    10 min

  4. Run the cup-of-water test

    Stand a microwave-safe mug holding about a cup of cold tap water in the middle of the turntable and run one minute at full power. It should come out noticeably warm. Water still cold after a full minute means the oven is not producing microwaves, which puts you firmly on the inverter and magnetron reading. Never test by running the oven empty, because with nothing absorbing the energy you can damage the magnetron doing it.

    8 minTools: Microwave-safe mug

    Never run a microwave with nothing inside it.

  5. Clear the vents and check the clearances

    Vacuum the grilles on the back, sides and top with a brush attachment, take anything stored on top of the oven off it, and make sure there is a clear gap behind. Built-in and over-range installations have specified clearances in the installation instructions, and a microwave squeezed into a cabinet opening without them will overheat repeatedly however healthy its components are.

    15 minTools: Vacuum with brush attachment

  6. Listen for the cooling fan while it runs

    With a cup of water inside and a short cook going, stand close and listen past the turntable motor for a steady fan note. Silence from the fan means the magnetron is running uncooled, which trips its protection and damages it over time. That is a repair inside the cabinet rather than anything you can adjust, and it is worth reporting when you book.

    5 min

  7. Stop at the case and get a quote

    If manual cooking fails, the water stays cold, or there is no fan, the rest of the job is behind the case on the high-voltage side. Take your full model number off the sticker inside the door frame or on the back and get a quote before anything is ordered. Weigh it against a replacement, because on many countertop Panasonic models those two numbers end up close together.

    15 min

    Do not open the cabinet to investigate. There is nothing in there you can safely check without training and a discharge tool.

How to reset it

Unplug the oven at the socket, leave it at least five minutes and plug it back in, keeping the door open while it stands so the cavity cools at the same time. That covers the stored-fault case and the overheat case together. A code that returns immediately on a cold oven with nothing running is a hardware fault and more resets will not move it. Give the reset one attempt, then let the cup-of-water test do the real work, since it tells you more than anything else available without opening the case.

It still shows H97

If cooling it down changed nothing and the water test failed, the microwave generating circuit is at fault and the question becomes whether to repair it. The parts are the inverter board at roughly $100 to $250 and the magnetron at roughly $70 to $180, and nobody can tell you which has gone without opening the case and testing the high-voltage side, because the code does not distinguish them. With a call-out and labor a repair usually comes to between $150 and $350. A comparable new Panasonic inverter microwave often costs about the same, which is why many shops will tell you over the phone that a countertop model is not worth a visit. That calculation flips for a built-in or over-range oven, where replacing means matching a cabinet opening and paying someone to fit it, and a $250 repair against $600 plus installation is an easy decision. Two things to do before you choose. Find your purchase date and check the warranty, because Panasonic magnetrons frequently carry parts cover running years beyond the general warranty on the rest of the oven, and people pay for repairs that were covered. And insist the technician confirms the failed component before ordering, rather than fitting an inverter board on the strength of the code, because H97 and H98 are not separated by any documentation I could find and the code on its own does not name a part. If the oven is out of warranty, sits on a counter, and the quote is more than about half the price of a new one, replacing it is the sensible answer.

Differences between models

No source I could find separates H97 from H98 on Panasonic microwaves, which matters if anyone quotes you for a specific part based on the code alone. Third-party repair references describe the two in almost identical language as a magnetron or inverter circuit failure caught by the self-diagnostic system, while Panasonic's own troubleshooting page groups H97 and H98 among codes tied to a cavity too hot from previous use to run an automatic program. Those readings do not reconcile, and the difference may be model generation or context. Your own operating manual, and Panasonic customer service with the model number in hand, outrank any code chart here. Watch out too for charts covering Panasonic air conditioners, which use H97 and H98 for entirely unrelated faults.

When to stop and call someone

Call once the water test fails, because everything past that is inside the case and on the high-voltage side. Give them your full model number and say the oven failed a cup-of-water test, which is the single most useful thing you can report and saves a diagnostic step. Ask for a quote before parts are ordered, and ask them to confirm which component failed rather than fitting one on the code alone. Check the magnetron warranty before you pay anything. And stop using the oven immediately, rather than waiting for an appointment, if the door sits crooked, the seal is damaged, or you see arcing inside.

Questions people ask

What does H97 mean on a Panasonic microwave?

It is a self-diagnostic code from the circuit that generates the microwaves, meaning the inverter board and the magnetron on an inverter model. Panasonic's own material also groups it with a cavity too hot to run an automatic program, which is entirely different. A manual cook and a cup-of-water test will tell you which one you have.

Is H97 different from H98?

Not in any documentation I could find. Third-party references describe them in nearly identical terms and Panasonic's own troubleshooting page lists them together. Treat them as the same investigation rather than assuming one names a particular part, and be cautious if a repairer quotes for a specific component purely on the basis of the code.

Can I fix H97 myself?

No. The high-voltage capacitor inside a microwave stores enough charge to kill and holds it after the oven is unplugged, so the case stays shut. Letting the oven cool, clearing the vents and running a water test are the things you can safely do, and they are all on this page. Everything else needs a technician with a discharge tool.

Can I keep using the microwave with H97 showing?

If manual cooking works and only automatic programs are refused, the oven is functioning and you can use it while the cavity cools down. If it fails the cup-of-water test it is not heating and there is nothing to use. Stop using it entirely if the door is damaged or you can see arcing in the cavity.

Why did H97 appear out of nowhere?

Magnetrons and inverter boards usually fail abruptly rather than declining. The other frequent trigger is heat, from a run of meals back to back or from vents clogged with dust or covered by something stored on top of the oven. Check the vents and the clearances before concluding a component has died.

Is it worth repairing an H97 fault?

On a countertop oven, often not: $150 to $350 of parts and labor against a comparable new inverter microwave at a similar price. On a built-in or over-range unit it usually is, because replacement means matching a cabinet opening and paying for fitting. Check whether the magnetron is still under its own extended warranty first.
