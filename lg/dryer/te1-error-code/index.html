LG dryer tE1 error code: what it means and how to fix it

Last reviewed 2026-10-15 · LG dryer

What it means

tE1 means the control board is reading a temperature from the dryer's thermistor that it does not believe. The thermistor is the small temperature sensor that tells the board how hot the air in the dryer is, and tE1 is raised when that reading is out of range, usually because the sensor has gone open circuit or its wiring has come loose. Start with a power reset, because LG says a reset clears a fair number of these, then check the exhaust vent for restriction before you open anything up. If it comes back, the sensor and its connector are the parts to test.

Safety first

Testing or replacing the thermistor means taking a panel off a machine that runs on 240 volt mains. Unplug the dryer at the wall before any panel comes off, and do not rely on the Power button. On a gas model, close the gas shutoff valve behind the dryer before moving it, and leave every gas component alone: burner, valve and igniter work goes to a licensed technician. Never bypass or jumper a thermal cut-out or thermal fuse to get a cycle to run.

Why an LG dryer shows TE1

These are the causes in the order you are most likely to hit them. Work down the list, do not skip to the expensive one.

  1. The thermistor has failed openmost casesWith the dryer unplugged, pull the connector off the thermistor and measure across its two terminals on the ohms range. An open circuit, meaning the meter never settles and reads infinite, is a dead sensor. A good one reads a steady resistance at room temperature that falls smoothly as you warm it with your hand. The target figure for your model is on the service sheet inside the cabinet.
  2. Loose, chafed or corroded wiring at the sensor connectormost casesThe sensor itself measures fine on the bench but the fault returns in the machine. Look at the connector for a pin pushed back out of its housing, green corrosion, or a wire that has rubbed through against the blower housing. Wiggle the harness with the meter connected across the sensor and watch for the reading flickering.
  3. A restricted exhaust vent driving real temperatures out of rangecommonThe dryer also runs long, clothes come out hot and still damp, and you may have seen a D80 or D90 code before this one. A duct that cannot clear hot air lets the drum run hotter than the control expects, and a sensor reporting that honestly can still be read as out of range.
  4. A transient control glitch that a reset clearscommonThe code appeared once, the dryer had been running normally, and nothing else is obviously wrong. LG's own guidance is to try a reset first. If a five minute power cut makes it go away and it does not come back over the next several loads, that is what it was.
  5. Heating element or thermal cut-out failure producing an impossible readingoccasionalThe drum turns but there is no heat at all, alongside the tE1. A heater that never comes on leaves the sensor reporting ambient temperature right through a heated cycle, which the control can also treat as a sensor fault.
  6. Main control board faultrareA thermistor that measures correctly, a clean connector, a clear vent and tE1 on every cycle regardless. Check the harness end to end before condemning the board, because a broken wire somewhere along the run looks exactly like this and costs a fraction as much.

Fix it step by step

  1. Power the dryer down properly and restart

    Press Power to turn the dryer off, then unplug it at the wall or switch off its breaker and leave it for five minutes. The board holds residual charge and a quick off-and-on frequently does not clear a stored fault. Restore power and run a short heated cycle with a couple of damp towels. LG's own first recommendation for tE1 is a reset, and it genuinely works often enough to be worth the five minutes.

    8 min

  2. Clean the lint screen and check the exhaust duct

    Pull the lint screen, wash the mesh in warm soapy water to strip the fabric softener film, dry it and refit it. Then pull the dryer out, disconnect the duct and check it from the machine to the outside wall cap, including whether the flap outside swings freely. A restricted vent makes the dryer run hotter than designed and is the cheapest thing on this list to rule out.

    25 minTools: Vacuum, vent brush, nut driver

  3. Unplug and open the cabinet

    Unplug the dryer at the wall. On a gas model, close the gas shutoff valve behind it first and move the machine without straining the flexible gas connector. On most LG dryers the thermistor sits on the blower housing or the heater duct inside the cabinet and is reached by taking the rear panel off, which is a row of hex head screws around its edge. The wiring diagram is usually folded inside the cabinet and tells you which connector you are looking for.

    20 minTools: 1/4 inch nut driver, Phillips screwdriver

    Unplug at the wall before removing any panel. On a gas dryer, close the gas shutoff valve before moving the machine.

  4. Inspect the sensor and its connector before testing anything

    Look at the thermistor where it clips to the housing and follow its two wires back. You are looking for a connector that has worked loose, a terminal backed out of its plastic housing, green or white corrosion, a wire chafed against sheet metal, or a sensor body cracked or knocked off its mount. Half of all tE1 faults are visible at this stage and cost nothing to fix.

    15 minTools: Flashlight

  5. Measure the thermistor and watch it respond to heat

    Unplug the connector and put a multimeter on ohms across the sensor's two terminals. Note the reading, then cup the sensor in your hand or hold a hairdryer near it on low for thirty seconds and watch the number. These sensors have a negative temperature coefficient, so the resistance must fall steadily as the sensor warms and climb back as it cools. An open circuit, a dead short, or a reading that never moves means a failed sensor. Compare the room temperature figure with the service sheet in the cabinet for your model.

    20 minTools: Multimeter

    Make sure the dryer is still unplugged. Never take a resistance reading on a live circuit.

  6. Replace the thermistor if it fails the test

    Order the thermistor for your exact model number from the sticker inside the door opening. It unclips or unscrews from the housing with one screw and the connector pushes on one way round. Route the new wiring exactly where the old ran, clipped away from the blower housing so it cannot chafe, and refit the rear panel before restoring power.

    30 minTools: Nut driver, replacement thermistor

  7. Test with a full heated cycle

    Plug the dryer back in, reopen the gas valve if you closed it, and run a complete heated cycle rather than a short one. tE1 often takes several minutes of heating to reappear, so a two minute test proves nothing. Check that the clothes come out properly dry and that the cycle time is back to normal.

    50 min

How to reset it

Press Power to turn the dryer off, then unplug it at the wall or switch the breaker off and wait a full five minutes before restoring power. The five minutes matters, because the control board holds charge and a quick cycle often leaves the stored fault in place. Then run a short heated cycle with a few damp towels to test. If tE1 returns on the next load, the reset has only hidden it and the sensor circuit needs checking properly.

It still shows TE1

If the reset did not hold, the vent is clear and a new thermistor has not fixed it, work through the remaining possibilities in cost order. First, check the harness rather than the sensor. Unplug the dryer, disconnect the thermistor connector at both the sensor end and the control board end, and measure continuity along each of the two wires. A wire broken inside its insulation, usually where the harness passes a sharp edge or flexes near the blower, reads open on the meter and looks perfect to the eye. This costs nothing but twenty minutes and it catches a real share of repeat tE1 faults. Second, confirm the dryer is actually heating. Run a cycle and feel the exhaust: if the drum turns but nothing gets warm, a failed heating element or a blown thermal cut-out leaves the sensor reporting room temperature right through a heated cycle, and the control can read that as a sensor fault. A thermal cut-out does not blow for no reason, so if you find one open, the restricted vent that killed it still needs clearing or you will do it again. Third, if you have a gas dryer that tumbles without ever lighting, stop and call a licensed gas technician rather than investigating the burner yourself. Fourth, only when the sensor measures correctly, the harness has continuity end to end, the vent is clear and the heater works is the main control board a reasonable suspect, and that is where the bill jumps: a board plus labor usually lands between $250 and $400. On a dryer more than about eight years old that is the point to weigh the repair against replacement, especially if the machine is also drying slowly or throwing duct codes.

Differences between models

LG uses several thermistor codes and they are not interchangeable. tE1 and tE3 both point at thermistor circuits that have gone out of range, while tE2 generally indicates a shorted sensor rather than an open one, and the sensor each code refers to can differ between a vented dryer and a heat pump or condensing model. Sources outside LG disagree about whether tE1 is the inlet or the outlet sensor, so use the wiring diagram inside your own cabinet rather than a generic answer to identify which sensor the connector belongs to. On heat pump models the sensors sit in the refrigerant circuit area, which is not DIY territory.

When to stop and call someone

Call a technician if you are not comfortable taking the rear panel off a 240 volt appliance or using a multimeter, because the rest of this job needs both. Call one straight away if you have a gas dryer that tumbles without lighting, since burner and gas valve work is licensed trade only. Call if the thermistor measures correctly, the harness has continuity and the vent is clear, because the next step is board level diagnosis. And on a heat pump or condensing LG dryer, where the sensors sit in the sealed refrigerant circuit area, treat tE1 as a service call from the start.

Questions people ask

What is a thermistor on an LG dryer?

It is a small temperature sensor, usually clipped to the blower housing or the heater duct, that reports air temperature to the control board as a changing electrical resistance. The board uses it to decide when to switch the heater on and off. When the reading goes outside the range the board expects, you get tE1.

Can I still use the dryer with a tE1 code?

Usually not, because the dryer stops the cycle rather than heating without a temperature reading it trusts. That caution is deliberate: running a heater with no reliable temperature feedback is how things overheat. If it does still tumble, it will be on no heat, so you are air-drying at best.

Will a reset fix tE1?

Sometimes, and it is LG's own first suggestion, so it is worth the five minutes. Power off, unplug or trip the breaker, wait five full minutes, restore power and run a heated cycle. If the code stays away over several loads it was a glitch. If it returns, the reset has not fixed anything and the sensor circuit needs testing.

Can a blocked vent cause tE1?

It can contribute. A restricted duct keeps hot air in the drum and pushes real temperatures above what the control expects, which can be read as an abnormal sensor reading. If you have seen D80, D90 or D95 recently, or clothes come out hot and damp after a long cycle, clear the duct before you buy any part.

How much does a tE1 repair cost?

The thermistor itself is typically $15 to $45 and is a one-screw part if you are willing to take the back panel off, so a DIY fix is cheap. A technician doing the same job usually charges $150 to $320 including the call-out. If the diagnosis lands on the main control board instead, expect $250 to $400.

Is tE1 the same as tE2 or tE3?

They are related but not identical. tE1 and tE3 both indicate a thermistor reading out of range, typically an open circuit or a sensor that is not responding, while tE2 generally points at a shorted sensor. Which physical sensor each refers to varies by model, so check the wiring diagram inside your own dryer.

Why did tE1 appear suddenly?

Thermistors usually fail abruptly rather than drifting: the element cracks, or a wire that has been rubbing against sheet metal for years finally breaks through. The other common trigger is work behind the machine, where moving the dryer tugs a harness and pulls a terminal out of its connector, which shows up on the very next cycle.
