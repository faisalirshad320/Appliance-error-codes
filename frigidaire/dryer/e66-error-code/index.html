Frigidaire dryer E66 error code: what it means and how to fix it

Last reviewed 2026-10-15 · Frigidaire dryer

What it means

E66 on a Frigidaire dryer is a fault in the heating circuit's thermal protection, most commonly an open thermal limiter at the blower or heater housing. A thermal limiter is a one-shot safety device: once it opens, it stays open and the dryer tumbles without heat until it is replaced. The important part is that a limiter almost never fails on its own. It opens because the machine overheated, and the overwhelming cause of that is restricted airflow through a blocked lint filter, a crushed transition duct or a clogged vent run. Fit a new limiter without clearing the vent and you will be fitting another one within weeks.

Safety first

An electric dryer runs on a 240-volt circuit and a gas dryer has a live gas connection; both are inside the cabinet you are about to open. Switch the dryer off at its breaker and, on a gas model, close the shutoff valve at the appliance before removing any panel. Never bypass, jumper or defeat a thermal limiter or a high-limit thermostat to get heat back. They exist because a restricted dryer can start a fire, and this code is the system telling you that it nearly did.

Why a Frigidaire dryer shows E66

These are the causes in the order you are most likely to hit them. Work down the list, do not skip to the expensive one.

  1. A restricted vent run that overheated the dryer and opened the limitermost casesPull the duct off the back of the dryer and look inside both the duct and the dryer outlet. A felt-like lint mat lining the duct, or a hood flap outside that no longer opens freely, is your answer. If the dryer heats normally with the duct disconnected and fails with it attached, this is settled.
  2. A crushed or kinked flexible transition duct behind the machinemost casesPull the dryer out and look at the four feet of duct between the machine and the wall. Foil or vinyl flex squashed flat against the wall, or coiled into two extra loops because the dryer sits too close, cuts airflow enough to trip a limiter even when the rest of the run is clean.
  3. A thermal limiter that is genuinely open with no airflow fault behind itcommonBreaker off, panel open, meter across the limiter terminals on the continuity range: open circuit confirms it. This is the component the code is reporting. The question is always whether something caused it, and a clean vent plus a clean filter housing is what lets you conclude it failed on age.
  4. A blocked lint filter or a lint-packed filter housingcommonHold the lint screen up and run water onto it. If the water pools rather than running straight through, fabric softener residue has sealed the mesh. Also shine a light down the filter slot itself: years of lint bypassing a poorly seated screen builds a plug in the housing you cannot see from above.
  5. A failed blower wheel or a slipped belt reducing airflow inside the machineoccasionalThe duct and the filter are clean and you still feel almost no air at the vent hood with the dryer running. A blower wheel that has cracked loose on its hub rattles and moves nothing. This is an internal airflow failure rather than a ducting one.
  6. Wiring in the heater circuit, or the heater relay on the control boardoccasionalThe limiter tests good, the thermostats test good, the vent is clear, and the dryer still posts E66 and will not heat. Burnt or discolored terminals at the heater box are visible evidence. Some published code tables read E66 as a heater relay fault on the board, and this is the scenario where that reading fits.

Fix it step by step

  1. Clean the lint screen properly, not just the surface

    Pull the screen, peel the lint off, then take it to a sink and run water onto the mesh. Water that pools instead of passing straight through means fabric softener residue has sealed it, which is invisible and chokes airflow badly. Scrub it with hot water and a nylon brush until water runs through freely, and let it dry fully before refitting. Then vacuum down the filter slot itself with a crevice tool or a dryer lint brush.

    15 minTools: Nylon brush, vacuum with crevice tool or long lint brush

  2. Disconnect the vent duct and run a heat test

    Pull the dryer forward, slacken the clamp and pull the duct off the outlet at the back. Start a timed dry cycle on high heat with the duct off and the dryer pulled clear of the wall, and feel the air at the outlet after three or four minutes. Heat with the duct off and no heat with it on tells you the fault is in the venting, not the machine. Run this test for a few minutes only, and do not leave the dryer venting into the room as a workaround.

    15 minTools: Nut driver or screwdriver for the clamp

    Do not run a gas dryer unvented for more than a brief test. Combustion products belong outside.

  3. Clear the whole vent run and the outside hood

    Feed a dryer vent brush on flexible rods through the duct from the dryer end, working it in and out, then do the same from outside. Pull the lint out rather than pushing it along. Go outside and check the hood: the flap should swing freely and the opening should not be matted with lint, blocked by a bird's nest, or screened with mesh, which is a common and illegal-in-most-codes modification that clogs within months. While it is apart, count the elbows; every 90-degree bend cuts the effective run.

    45 minTools: Dryer vent brush kit with rods, vacuum, ladder if the hood is high

  4. Replace or reroute a crushed transition duct

    The flexible duct between the dryer and the wall should be as short and straight as you can make it, with no squashing. Rigid or semi-rigid aluminum is far better than foil or vinyl flex, which crush flat the moment the dryer is pushed back. Leave four to six inches of clearance behind the machine. If you have to coil surplus duct to make it fit, you have too much duct, not too little room.

    25 minTools: Semi-rigid aluminum transition duct, foil tape, two clamps, nut driver

    Do not use sheet metal screws in a duct joint. The screw tips catch lint and build a plug.

  5. Isolate the machine before opening anything

    Switch the dryer off at its breaker, not at the control panel. On an electric dryer this is a 240-volt circuit and the terminal block is live right where you are about to work. On a gas dryer, also close the shutoff valve at the appliance and leave it closed until you are finished. Check with a non-contact tester at the terminal block before touching anything if you have one.

    5 minTools: Non-contact voltage tester

    240 volts on an electric dryer, live gas on a gas dryer. Isolate both before any panel comes off.

  6. Test the thermal limiter and the thermostats for continuity

    With the power off, remove the rear panel, or the front and toe panel on some models, and find the heater housing or blower housing. The thermal limiter and the high-limit thermostat are small discs or rectangles mounted on it with two spade terminals each. Pull one wire off each and measure across the terminals on the continuity or ohms range. A good one reads closed; an open circuit means it has tripped and will not come back. Replace the limiter and the thermostat as a pair rather than one at a time.

    40 minTools: Nut driver set, Phillips screwdriver, multimeter, flashlight

    Breaker off and, on gas models, gas off before the panel comes off. Never jumper across a limiter to test for heat.

  7. Reset and run a full test cycle with the vent connected

    Reassemble, reconnect the duct properly with clamps and foil tape, restore power and run a full timed dry on high heat with a few damp towels in the drum. Go outside after ten minutes and put a hand at the vent hood: you want a strong warm flow that you can feel from a foot away. Weak flow at the hood means the vent is still restricted and the new limiter is already being cooked.

    45 min

How to reset it

There is no button sequence that clears this one in any useful sense. A thermal limiter is a one-shot device: once it has opened, it does not reset, cool down and come back, and cycling the power only clears the code from the display until the control tests the circuit again. Switch the dryer off at its breaker for five minutes and back on to clear the stored fault after you have made the repair, then run a full timed dry on high heat to confirm. If E66 reappears during that cycle, the circuit is still open and nothing has been fixed.

It still shows E66

If you have replaced the limiter, cleaned the vent and the code is back, something is still cooking the heating circuit, and the sequence from here is about finding what rather than fitting another part. First, measure the airflow you actually have rather than the airflow you assume. Run the dryer and hold a hand at the outside hood: a healthy machine pushes a strong warm stream you can feel standing back from it. If the flow is weak with a clean duct, the restriction is inside the machine, and the two candidates are a lint plug in the blower housing and a blower wheel that has cracked loose on its hub. Pull the duct off the dryer outlet and feel the air right there: strong at the machine and weak at the hood means ducting, weak at both means the blower. Second, check the run itself against what it should be. Every 90-degree elbow counts for several feet of straight duct, and a long run with three or four bends, or a flexible duct used for the whole run instead of rigid pipe, will trip limiters indefinitely no matter how often you clean it. That is a ducting job, not an appliance repair, and it is the thing nobody wants to hear. Third, if airflow is genuinely good and the limiter opened anyway, the problem has moved to the heater circuit itself: a heating element that has sagged and is touching the housing, a shorted element drawing more than it should, or the heater relay on the control board sticking closed so the element keeps running when the control has told it to stop. That last one matches the reading some published code tables give for E66, and it is the scenario where the code points at the board rather than the vent. All three of those are technician work, and all three are the point where the cost jumps from parts money to real money. One honest caveat: Frigidaire's dryer code tables are not consistently published, and third-party lists disagree about E66, with the most authoritative-looking service references calling it an open thermal limiter circuit and several repair sites calling it a heater relay fault. The thermal limiter is the cheaper and more likely of the two, and testing it costs nothing but a meter, so test it first either way, then get the tech sheet from behind the toe panel before anyone orders a control board.

Differences between models

Frigidaire and Electrolux dryers sold in North America share a platform and an E-number fault scheme, so guidance for one usually transfers to the other of the same era. Within that scheme, the neighboring codes matter for context: there are separate codes for an open heater element and for a high-limit thermostat tripping repeatedly, which is why E66 being specifically about the thermal limiter circuit makes sense. Be aware that published meanings for E66 conflict: service-derived references describe an open thermal limiter circuit, covering the outlet limiter and, on gas models, the inlet limiter as well, while several repair sites describe a heating element relay fault on the main control board. Gas models have the extra inlet limiter that electric models do not, so a gas machine has one more device in the chain to test. On models without a digital display, this family of faults is reported through indicator light patterns and is only readable with the tech sheet.

When to stop and call someone

Call a technician if you are not comfortable working inside a 240-volt appliance, if you have a gas dryer and the inlet limiter is involved, or if the limiter has opened again after a vent clean and a replacement, because that means something is still overheating and the next tests are on live circuits. Frigidaire is the easy brand for this: parts are cheap and stocked everywhere, nearly every independent appliance technician works on them, and you are not tied to a thin authorized network, so expect competitive quotes and a visit within a few days in most of the country. Weigh that against what the machine is worth. A limiter and thermostat kit fitted by a technician typically runs $180 to $280 all in, which is sensible on almost any dryer. A heating element or a control board pushes past $350, and on a dryer over about eight years old that is a large share of a replacement. One call that is not an appliance technician at all: if your vent run is long, flexible for its whole length, or has several bends, what you need is a duct cleaning or a rerouting job, and a dryer vent specialist will fix the actual cause for less than a technician will charge to keep replacing the symptom.

Questions people ask

What does E66 mean on a Frigidaire dryer?

It flags a fault in the heating circuit's thermal protection, most commonly an open thermal limiter at the heater or blower housing. The limiter is a one-shot safety device that opens permanently when the dryer overheats. The code tells you the circuit is open. It does not tell you why, and the why is nearly always restricted airflow.

Can I still use the dryer with E66 showing?

It will usually tumble without heating, so clothes come out damp after a full cycle. There is no benefit to running it and there is a real reason not to: the limiter opened because the machine overheated, and the condition that caused that, usually a lint-packed vent, is a genuine fire risk that does not go away by itself.

Can I just bypass the thermal limiter to get heat back?

No. Jumpering a limiter removes the only thing standing between a restricted dryer and a fire, and dryer fires caused by lint and blocked venting are not a theoretical risk. If a limiter has opened, replace it and fix what made it open. No competent technician will do anything else, and neither should you.

Why did E66 appear suddenly when the dryer was fine last week?

Lint accumulates in the vent gradually until the run crosses a threshold, and then the machine overheats on a single heavy load and the limiter opens in one event. It looks sudden and it is not. The other common trigger is the dryer being pushed back against the wall after cleaning, crushing the transition duct flat.

How do I know whether the vent or the dryer is at fault?

Pull the duct off the back of the dryer and run a short heat cycle with the machine clear of the wall. If it heats normally with the duct disconnected and fails with it attached, the venting is your fault. Keep the test brief, and never run a gas dryer unvented for more than a few minutes.

Does E66 definitely mean the thermal limiter is bad?

Not definitely. The best-sourced reading of this code is an open thermal limiter circuit, and that is where to start because testing it costs nothing but a multimeter. Some published tables instead read E66 as a heater relay fault on the control board. Test the limiter and thermostats first, then get your model's tech sheet before anyone orders a board.

How much does it cost to fix a Frigidaire dryer E66?

Done yourself, a limiter and thermostat kit is $15 to $70 plus a couple of hours. A technician typically charges $180 to $280 for the same job. If a heating element or control board turns out to be involved, budget $300 to $400 and think hard about the machine's age before agreeing to it.

Will cleaning the vent stop E66 coming back?

It is the single most effective thing you can do, but only if you clean the whole run rather than the first few feet. Brush the duct from both ends, check the outside hood flap swings freely, replace crushed flexible duct with semi-rigid aluminum, and leave clearance behind the machine. Then clean the run annually, not when it fails.
