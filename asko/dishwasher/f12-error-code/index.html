Asko dishwasher F12 error code: why it will not fill and how to fix it

Last reviewed 2026-10-15 · Asko dishwasher

What it means

F12 on an Asko dishwasher is a water inlet fault. The machine opened its fill valve, waited for the water level to reach the point the program needs, and timed out before it got there. Four times out of five the restriction is outside the machine: a supply valve that is only part open, a kinked fill hose, or the fine mesh screen at the hose connection packed with grit. The tell is a cycle that starts normally, runs for a minute or two, then beeps and stops with the tub nearly dry.

Why an Asko dishwasher shows F12

These are the causes in the order you are most likely to hit them. Work down the list, do not skip to the expensive one.

  1. The supply valve under the sink is closed or only part openmost casesFind the small valve on the hot supply under the sink and open it fully. A quarter-turn lever must sit in line with the pipe, not across it. If anyone has worked under that sink recently, or there was a leak scare, somebody turned it down and did not turn it back.
  2. The inlet mesh screen at the hose connection is cloggedmost casesClose the valve and undo the fill hose at the machine end. There is a fine mesh screen set into the inlet port or the hose fitting. If it looks brown, furred or scaled rather than bright, this is your fault. It is the single most common thing a technician finds on a fill complaint.
  3. Household water pressure is genuinely too lowcommonWith the hose off, put the loose end in a bucket and open the valve for five seconds. You want a strong jet. A weak dribble means the problem is upstream in your plumbing and the dishwasher is reporting it correctly. Check whether it only happens when a shower is running.
  4. A flood-protection inlet hose has trippedoccasionalIf your hose is a thick one with a plastic housing at the tap end, it has a safety valve inside that latches shut after a sudden flow surge and does not reset itself. The symptom is no water at all rather than slow water, and the housing often shows a small colored indicator through a window when it has fired.
  5. The fill valve itself has failedoccasionalValve fully open, screen clean, strong flow into a bucket, and still F12. Either the solenoid is open circuit or the valve is mechanically stuck shut. This is the point at which the job stops being free.
  6. Water is leaving as fast as it comes inoccasionalYou can hear water running continuously but the level never builds. A drain hose that terminates lower than the tub floor with no high loop siphons the machine empty while it fills, so the level sensor never sees its target. Check that the hose rises to just under the countertop before it drops to the waste.
  7. The water level sensor, its hose, or the control boardrareThe tub visibly fills to a normal depth and the machine still calls a fill fault. The water is arriving; the control cannot see it. Everything upstream has been proven good by this point.

Fix it step by step

  1. Open the supply valve fully and check the hose run

    Open the cabinet under the sink and find the small shutoff on the line feeding the dishwasher. Turn it fully counterclockwise, or set a lever type in line with the pipe. Then follow the braided fill hose back to the machine and look for a tight bend, a flat spot where the cabinet or a leveling foot has pinched it, or a loop trapped behind the drain hose. This clears the code more often than every other cause put together.

    8 minTools: Flashlight

  2. Cancel the program and reset the control

    Cancel the running program at the panel, then switch the dishwasher off at its breaker for two full minutes and back on. The control stores the fault, so a machine that now has a perfectly good water supply will keep showing F12 until it is cleared. Restart on the shortest program you have rather than a full wash, so you find out in minutes.

    8 min

  3. Test the flow into a bucket

    Close the supply valve. Undo the fill hose at the valve end with a wrench, hold the loose end in a bucket, and open the valve for five seconds. You should half fill a mop bucket with a strong jet. If you get a weak stream or a trickle, stop working on the dishwasher: the restriction is in the house plumbing, and a plumber is the right call. Reconnect snugly, not gorilla tight, and check for weeping.

    12 minTools: Adjustable wrench, bucket, towel

    Close the supply valve first or you will take mains pressure in the face when the fitting comes loose.

  4. Clean the inlet mesh screen

    With the valve still closed, undo the fill hose at the machine end. Expect a cupful of water. Inside the inlet port, or set into the hose fitting, is a fine mesh screen. Pick it out with needle-nose pliers, rinse it under a tap, scrub it with an old toothbrush and push it back in squarely. If it is scaled rather than just dirty, soak it in white vinegar for ten minutes first. A screen refitted crooked leaks, so take the extra few seconds.

    15 minTools: Needle-nose pliers, old toothbrush, white vinegar, towel

  5. Check for a tripped flood-protection hose

    If the fill hose has a bulky plastic housing at the tap end, that is a safety device that latches shut after a flow surge and does not reopen on its own. Some have a visible indicator window that changes color when they have fired. A tripped one gives you no water at all. These are not rebuildable: the hose assembly is replaced as a unit, and it is a cheap part compared with anything else on this page.

    10 minTools: Adjustable wrench, towel

  6. Rule out a siphon on the drain side

    Pull the machine forward and look at where the corrugated drain hose goes. It should climb to just below the countertop and be clipped there before dropping to the sink waste or disposal. A hose that runs flat along the floor into a low connection will quietly siphon the tub empty while the fill valve is still running, and the machine reports a fill fault for what is really a drain-side plumbing error. A cable tie fixes it.

    15 minTools: Cable tie, flashlight

  7. Check the fill valve electrically

    Switch the dishwasher off at its breaker. The fill valve sits in the base of the machine behind the lower kickplate, where the supply hose enters. Take the kickplate off, pull the connector from the solenoid and measure across its two terminals on the ohms range. An open circuit means a dead solenoid and a valve that will never open. Do not guess at what the reading should be: check the service sheet for your model rather than any figure you find online.

    25 minTools: Torx screwdriver, multimeter, flashlight

    Breaker off before the kickplate comes off. Mains wiring and the fill valve share that space.

How to reset it

Cancel the program at the panel, then switch the dishwasher off at its breaker, or unplug it if it is cord-connected under the sink, for a full two minutes. Restore power and start the shortest program you have. The two minutes matters: the control holds the stored fault, and a quick off-on routinely leaves F12 on the display even after the water supply has been fixed. If the code returns within about two minutes of restarting, the machine still is not getting its water and you have not found the restriction yet.

It still shows F12

If the supply valve is fully open, the screen is clean, the bucket test gives a strong jet and the drain hose has a proper high loop, the supply side is proven and the fault is inside the machine. Work through it cheapest first. Start by listening with the door shut at the beginning of a program: a clear hiss of water entering that stops after a couple of minutes tells you the valve is opening and the level is not being reached, which points at slow flow or a siphon rather than a dead valve. No hiss at all means no water is being admitted, and then the question is whether the valve is not being told to open or cannot open. Next, switch off at the breaker, take the kickplate off and look at the connector on the fill valve. Green corrosion or a terminal pushed back out of the housing is a real and free failure in a cabinet that has ever had a leak, and it is worth two minutes before anyone talks about parts. Then measure across the solenoid with a multimeter: an open circuit confirms a failed valve, which is a $45 to $110 part and the cheapest real repair on this list. If the solenoid reads correctly and still never energizes when the program calls for water, the fault moves to the harness or the main board, and that is where the cost jumps past the value of an older machine. Last, the awkward case worth knowing about: a tub that visibly fills to a normal depth and still throws F12. That is the level sensing side, not the water side, and it means the pressure chamber or its small hose is blocked with sludge or the sensor has drifted. A technician can test that in minutes and you cannot easily test it at all, so this is the point to stop and call rather than keep buying parts.

Differences between models

Asko's F-number fault codes are not published in a single consistent public list, and third-party code tables on the web disagree with one another on several numbers, F12 included. The water inlet reading used on this page is the one supported by Asko parts documentation and by the pattern owners actually report, which is a cycle that starts and then stops a minute or two in with a dry tub. A small number of lists give F12 a different meaning entirely, so if nothing on this page matches what your machine is doing, check the use and care guide for your exact model number before buying anything. Asko also uses F4 and F2 for neighboring fill and overfill faults, and on level-controlled models these overlap, so treat the fill group as a family rather than chasing one number.

When to stop and call someone

Call a technician once you have strong flow into a bucket, a clean screen and a proper drain high loop, and the machine still will not fill, because the remaining tests mean working with the machine live. Factor the brand in before you commit. Asko has a noticeably thinner service network in the United States than the mainstream brands, so expect a longer wait for an appointment, a higher call-out charge, and a fair chance that the fill valve or sensor is not on the van and has to be ordered. A single visit plus a valve typically lands between $200 and $350; a two-visit job because the part had to come in lands higher and sometimes a week later. That changes the repair-or-replace math on an older machine in a way it would not on a Bosch or a Whirlpool, so get the quote in writing. Call a plumber rather than an appliance technician if the bucket test gave a weak dribble, or if the fill only fails when someone is running a shower, because that fault is in your house supply and an appliance engineer will charge you to say so.

Questions people ask

What does F12 mean on an Asko dishwasher?

It is a water inlet fault: the machine called for water and did not reach the level it needs within the time it allows, so it stopped the program. It is a report that the water did not arrive fast enough, not a diagnosis of the reason. Most of the time the restriction is in the supply valve, the hose or the inlet screen rather than inside the dishwasher.

Can I still use the dishwasher while it is showing F12?

No. The machine halts rather than washing with too little water, so a cycle will not complete. There is no safety risk in leaving it sitting there, but check for leaks under the sink before you walk away, since a half-closed or weeping supply valve is one of the common causes and water under a cabinet does damage quietly.

Why did F12 appear suddenly when nothing changed?

Inlet screens clog gradually and then cross a threshold, so months of slow decline show up as one sudden failure. The other frequent trigger is work elsewhere in the house: a plumber closing a stopcock, a meter swap, or a mains repair that stirs up sediment and deposits it in your screen within a week of the job.

Does F12 mean the fill valve needs replacing?

Usually not. In most cases the valve is fine and the restriction is a part-closed supply valve, a kinked hose or a clogged screen. Only replace the valve after a bucket test shows strong flow and a multimeter shows an open circuit across the solenoid, because a new valve fitted to a restricted supply throws the same code on its first cycle.

How do I clear the F12 code after fixing the water supply?

Cancel the program, cut power at the breaker for two full minutes, then restore it and run the shortest cycle. The control stores the fault, so the code can stay on the panel even after the supply is perfect. If it comes back within about two minutes, water still is not reaching the machine and the restriction is still there.

The tub fills with water but I still get F12. What is going on?

Water is arriving but the control cannot see it reach the target level. Two things cause that: the tub is draining away as fast as it fills, usually because the drain hose has no high loop and is siphoning, or the water level sensing circuit is blocked or has drifted. Check the high loop first, since that costs nothing.

Is an Asko dishwasher worth repairing for a fill fault?

For the supply-side causes, yes, because they cost nothing but your time. For a fill valve at $45 to $110 plus labor, yes on a machine under about eight years old, since Asko tubs and racks generally outlive the valves. The thing to weigh is the parts wait and the higher call-out that come with a thinner US service network.
