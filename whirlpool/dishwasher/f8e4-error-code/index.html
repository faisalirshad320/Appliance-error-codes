Whirlpool dishwasher F8 E4 error code: what it means and how to fix it

Last reviewed 2026-10-15 · Whirlpool dishwasher

What it means

F8 E4 means the overfill float sitting in the base pan under your Whirlpool dishwasher has been lifted by water, so the control has declared a flood and shut the cycle down. The F8 half says the problem is with water level and the E4 half says specifically that water is where it should not be. The machine then runs its drain pump more or less continuously to protect your floor, and it will refuse to start a normal cycle until that pan is dry and the float has dropped back. There is usually a real leak behind it, and it is usually small: a loose hose clamp, a weeping door seal, or nothing more than too much detergent foaming over.

Safety first

Switch the dishwasher off at its breaker before you reach under the machine or remove the lower access panel. You are working with mains wiring and standing water in the same small space, and the motor and heating element connections are down there with the pan. Turn off the water supply too, usually a small valve under the sink, so a connection you disturb cannot flood the kitchen.

Why a Whirlpool dishwasher shows F8E4

These are the causes in the order you are most likely to hit them. Work down the list, do not skip to the expensive one.

  1. A small ongoing leak dripping into the base panmost casesDry the pan completely, run a short cycle, and look underneath with a flashlight while it runs. The usual sources, in order of how often they turn up: the water inlet connection at the valve, a drain hose clamp, the circulation pump or sump seal, and the door gasket at the lower corners. A drip every few seconds is enough to trip this code over one cycle.
  2. Too much detergent, or the wrong detergent, foaming overmost casesOpen the door mid-cycle and look for a tub full of foam rather than water. Anyone who has ever put hand dish soap in the dispenser will recognize this instantly. It also happens with over-dosed powder in soft water, a rinse aid cap left loose, or a pre-rinse with washing-up liquid still clinging to the dishes.
  3. Water already in the pan from an earlier leak that was never driedcommonYou fixed something, or a hose was knocked and reseated, and the code stayed. The float does not care that the leak has stopped; it only cares that it is still floating. If the pan is damp and the code persists after a genuine repair, this is it, and the fix is patience and towels.
  4. The float stuck in the raised position, or a failed float switchcommonPan bone dry, no leak anywhere, code still showing. Look at the float itself: a foam or plastic block in the base pan that should drop freely under its own weight. If it is wedged by debris, by a cable, or by the insulation blanket slipping down, it reads as a flood forever.
  5. The water inlet valve seeping while the machine is offoccasionalThe code appears after the dishwasher has sat unused overnight rather than during a cycle. Dry the pan, leave the machine off but with the water supply on, and check again in the morning. Water in the pan with the appliance switched off points squarely at the valve or the supply connection.
  6. A damaged float switch harness or a control board faultrareDry pan, free float, no leak, and the code survives a full reset. Check the connector and the wiring run to the switch for corrosion or a chafed wire before accepting a board diagnosis, because damp under a dishwasher wrecks connectors long before it wrecks boards.

Fix it step by step

  1. Kill the power and the water

    Switch off the dishwasher's breaker at the consumer unit, or unplug it if it plugs into a socket under the sink. Then close the small shut-off valve on the water line feeding it, usually under the sink next to the faucet supplies, turning it clockwise until it stops. Doing both now means nothing can refill or energize while you have your hands under the machine.

    6 min

    Confirm the power is actually off at the machine, not just at the control panel. Dishwasher breakers are often mislabeled.

  2. Remove the lower access panel and dry out the base pan

    The kick panel across the bottom front is held by two to four Phillips screws along its top edge, sometimes behind a separate decorative strip that unclips first. Lift it away and you are looking into the base pan. Soak the water up with towels and a sponge, and get right into the corners, because the float sits low and a shallow film is enough to keep it up. Finish with a dry towel left in there for twenty minutes.

    25 minTools: Phillips #2 screwdriver, towels, sponge, shallow tray

    Breaker off before the panel comes off. Mains connections for the motor and heater are in this compartment.

  3. Find the float and check it drops freely

    In the base pan there is a float, usually a block of foam or hollow plastic, riding on a vertical stem or a pivoted arm with a small switch underneath or beside it. Lift it with a finger and let go: it should fall back under its own weight with a small click from the switch. If it sticks, clear whatever is fouling it, often a slipped piece of the insulation blanket, a cable tie or a lump of grease, and work it up and down a few times.

    12 minTools: Flashlight

  4. Restore power and let the machine drain itself out

    With the pan dry and the float free, put the breaker back on but leave the water supply off for now. The dishwasher will usually run its drain pump for a while and then settle. Let it finish rather than interrupting it. Once it stops and the code clears, you know the float has dropped and the control is happy, which is the baseline you need before you can test for the leak.

    20 min

  5. Run a short cycle with no detergent and watch underneath

    Open the water supply again, leave the access panel off, and start the shortest cycle the machine has with an empty tub and nothing in the dispenser. Kneel with a flashlight and watch the base pan through the fill, the wash and the drain. You are looking for the first drip and where it lands. Check the four usual places in turn: the inlet connection at the valve on one side, the drain hose and its clamp, the underside of the sump and circulation pump, and the lower corners of the door.

    40 minTools: Flashlight, dry paper towel to dab suspect joints

    Keep hands clear of the motor and the heating element while the machine is running and live.

  6. Deal with suds if that is what you found

    If the tub filled with foam rather than water, stop the cycle and scoop the foam out. Pour a cup of cold water into the tub base to knock the remaining suds down, then run a rinse cycle with nothing in the dispenser, and repeat until the water runs clear. Then fix the cause: use dishwasher detergent only, never hand dish soap, cut the dose if you have soft water, and check the rinse aid cap is seated and not overfilled.

    35 minTools: Jug of cold water, cloth

  7. Tighten and reseat the connections you can reach

    With the power and water off again, check the inlet fitting at the valve for dampness and nip it up gently with an adjustable wrench, a quarter turn at most, because overtightening a brass fitting splits it. Check the drain hose clamp at the pump and at the disposer or standpipe end, and squeeze a spring clamp or snug a screw clamp. Run a paper towel around each joint afterwards; it finds seepage that your eye will not.

    25 minTools: Adjustable wrench, nut driver or pliers, paper towel

    Water supply off before you touch the inlet fitting.

  8. Check the door gasket and the bottom door seal

    Run a finger along the gasket around the door opening and look for a section that has gone flat, hardened or come adrift from its channel. Then look at the strip along the bottom edge of the door, which is a separate wiper on most Whirlpool models and is the single most common place a dishwasher dribbles onto its own base pan. Food debris caught under it holds the door open a fraction, which is enough.

    12 minTools: Flashlight, cloth

How to reset it

Turn the dishwasher off at its breaker, or unplug it, and leave it for five minutes before restoring power. On models with a visible cycle pad you can also try holding Cancel or Cancel and Drain and letting the machine complete its drain cycle. Either way, the reset will not stick while there is water under the float. Dry the pan first, then reset, in that order. If the machine starts up and immediately runs its drain pump again without filling, that is not a failed reset; it is the control doing exactly what it should because the float is still up.

It still shows F8E4

If the pan is genuinely dry, the float moves freely and F8 E4 keeps coming back, work through it in this order. First, make sure the pan really is dry, because this is where most people go wrong. The float sits at the lowest point and a film of water you can barely see will hold it up. Towel it out, then leave the access panel off and a fan blowing into the compartment for a few hours, or overnight if it has been leaking for a while. The insulation blanket around the tub also holds water like a sponge and drips back into the pan for a day after the leak itself has stopped, which is why a correct repair can look like a failed one. Second, if the pan dries and refills with the appliance switched off and the water on, you have a supply-side seep rather than a wash leak, and that is the inlet valve or its fitting. Dry it, leave the machine off overnight with the water on, and check in the morning: it is the cleanest test on this page and it saves pulling the machine out for nothing. Third, if it refills only during a cycle, you need to see it happen. Run the shortest cycle with the panel off and a flashlight, and if you still cannot spot the source, pull the dishwasher out from under the counter far enough to see the sides and the back, because a leak at the drain hose connection or the rear of the sump is invisible from the front. Take the kickboard, the screws holding the machine to the counter and the floor into account before you pull, and keep the hoses slack. Fourth, the expensive end. A weep from the sump seal or the circulation pump shaft seal shows as water appearing on the underside of the tub directly below the pump rather than running from a hose, and that is where the job turns into a pump or sump replacement, roughly $80 to $200 in parts plus most of an afternoon, or a technician visit in the $200 to $400 range. At that point, weigh it against the age of the machine. Finally, if nothing is leaking at all and the code survives a full reset with a dry pan and a free float, suspect the switch and its wiring before the board. The underside of a dishwasher is a brutal environment for a connector, and a corroded plug on a good switch reads exactly like a flood.

Differences between models

Whirlpool uses this two-part F and E code format across dishwashers built from roughly 2014 onward, and the same codes appear on Maytag, KitchenAid, Amana and Whirlpool-built Kenmore machines, because they come off the same platforms. On hidden-control models with no digital display, the code is shown by flashing lights instead: count the flashes of the Clean light for the first number and the Start light for the second, so four flashes of Start after eight of Clean is F8 E4. Some older Whirlpool dishwashers report base-pan water without a numeric code at all , running the drain pump and refusing to start, which is the same fault and the same fix. The float design varies: most are a foam block on a stem in the pan, a few use a pivoted arm.

When to stop and call someone

Call someone if you can see water arriving in the pan but cannot find where it comes from after running a cycle with the panel off, because the next step is pulling the machine out from under the counter with the plumbing and electrics still attached, and that goes wrong easily in a fitted kitchen. Call if the leak traces back to the sump or the circulation pump seal and you are not comfortable dropping the pump, since that means laying the machine on its back and dealing with the motor seal. Call a plumber rather than an appliance technician if the water turns out to be arriving from the disposer connection, the standpipe or the supply line rather than from the dishwasher itself, which happens more often than you would think. And stop and get help immediately if water is reaching the floor or the cabinet rather than staying in the pan, because a dishwasher leak into a kitchen floor costs far more to put right than any part on this list.

Questions people ask

Can I still use the dishwasher with an F8 E4 code?

No, and the machine will not let you. It holds the cycle and runs its drain pump to keep water off your floor, which is the control doing its job. Forcing it by repeatedly resetting means running a machine that is actively leaking into its own base, and the next thing to get wet is usually the cabinet floor. Find the leak first.

Why does the drain pump keep running on its own?

Because the float is up and the control has entered its flood response. The pump runs to clear water it assumes is accumulating, and it will keep going until the float drops or you cut the power. It is not a pump fault and it is not stuck in a loop. Dry the base pan and it stops.

I fixed the leak but F8 E4 is still showing. Why?

Almost certainly because the pan is still wet. The float only reports whether it is floating, not whether the leak is fixed, and the insulation blanket around the tub holds water and drips back for a day after a leak stops. Towel the pan out, leave the access panel off with a fan blowing in, give it a few hours, then reset.

Can too much detergent really cause F8 E4?

Yes, and it is one of the two most common causes. Foam escapes past the door seal and finds its way into the base pan, lifting the float even though nothing is broken. Hand dish soap in the dispenser does this instantly. Over-dosed powder in soft water and a loose rinse aid cap do it more slowly.

Why did F8 E4 appear suddenly when nothing changed?

Leaks under a dishwasher are small for a long time and then cross a threshold. A hose clamp relaxes over years, a door seal hardens a little each season, a sump gasket weeps a drop per cycle. The code is the moment the pan finally held enough water to lift the float, not the moment the leak started.

Where exactly is the float on a Whirlpool dishwasher?

In the base pan under the tub, reached by taking off the kick panel across the bottom front, not inside the tub where older dishwashers put theirs. It is a foam or hollow plastic block riding on a stem or arm with a small switch beneath it. Lift it and let go: it should drop freely and click.

Is it worth repairing?

Usually yes, and often for nothing. Most of these come down to a hose clamp, a door seal or a detergent habit, which cost between nothing and sixty dollars. The calculation only changes if the leak traces to the sump or circulation pump seal on a machine already eight or more years old, where a few hundred dollars of repair meets a comparably priced replacement.

What is the difference between F8 E4 and the other F8 codes?

They all concern water level but report different things. F8 E4 specifically means the float in the base pan has found water, so it is a leak or overflow. Other F8 sub-codes cover fill problems such as the machine not reaching its water level in time, which is the opposite situation. Read both halves of the code before you start.
